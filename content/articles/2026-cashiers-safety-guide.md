## Part 1: Learning the Terms

Before we look at the risks, let's break down the words scientists use.

**Thermal Paper** is the professional name for receipt paper. It's called "thermal" because it doesn't use liquid ink. Instead, the paper is coated in a chemical that turns black when the heated print head of your register touches it. No ink cartridge needed — the chemical is the ink.

**[Bisphenols](/articles/the-science-of-bisphenols)** are the family of chemicals that make this coating work. Think of them like an invisible chemical dust sitting on the very surface of the paper — right where your fingers touch.

**BPA (Bisphenol A)** is the most well-known member of this chemical family and was used in most thermal paper for decades.

**BPS (Bisphenol S)** is BPA's chemical cousin. When companies came under pressure for using BPA, many switched to BPS so they could label their paper "BPA-Free." The problem: BPS is now known to carry the same risks. A "BPA-Free" receipt almost always means "contains BPS instead."

## Part 2: What Bisphenols Do to Your Body — The "Fake Key" Problem

Your body uses hormones to send messages. Think of hormones like keys that fit into specific locks in your cells — telling your heart to beat, your brain to sleep, your body to grow.

Bisphenols are shaped almost identically to estrogen, one of your body's most important hormones. When these chemicals enter your bloodstream, they act like **fake keys** — they slide into the same locks and send messages your body never intended.

This is called **endocrine disruption**. "Endocrine" is simply the scientific word for your hormone system. When a chemical interferes with hormone signals over a long period, it can contribute to problems with reproductive health, thyroid function, weight management, and heart health.

## Part 3: The Facts — Why Cashiers Are at Higher Risk

### It Rubs Off Like Chalk

The bisphenol coating isn't bonded deeply into the paper — it sits on the surface. When you touch the shiny (printed) side, the chemical transfers to your fingers in as little as 2 seconds, just like chalk dust coming off a chalkboard.

### The Hand Sanitizer Problem — 100x More Absorption

This is the most important fact for 2026.

Your skin normally acts as a shield against chemicals. But hand sanitizer contains penetration enhancers — ingredients designed to help things absorb through skin quickly. These don't just help the sanitizer absorb. They turn your skin's shield into a doorway for everything on your hands, including bisphenols from receipts.

[Research shows](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24947840/) that handling receipts with wet or recently sanitized hands increases bisphenol absorption through the skin by up to **[100 times](/articles/how-much-bisphenol-exposure-is-too-much)** compared to dry hands.

The rule is simple: **dry hands before receipts.**

### The "BPA-Free" Myth

Don't let a "BPA-Free" label give you a false sense of security. Most BPA-Free thermal paper simply substitutes BPS — a chemical so structurally similar that researchers believe it carries identical health risks. [Studies confirm](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23164578/) BPS is just as potent an endocrine disruptor as BPA.

## Part 4: Your 3-Step Action Plan

You don't need to wait for a workplace policy change to start protecting yourself. Do these three things today.

### Step 1: The Scratch Test — Do This Right Now

Not sure if your store uses thermal paper? Take a receipt and scratch the shiny side quickly with your fingernail. If it leaves a dark gray or black streak, you are holding thermal paper with a bisphenol coating.

Now you know for certain — and you know to handle it with care for the rest of your shift.

### Step 2: The Sanitizer Strike — Do This for Your Health

For the rest of today, stop using hand sanitizer immediately before touching receipts. Use plain soap and cool water on your breaks instead. You are closing the doorway those chemicals use to enter your body.

If you must use sanitizer, wait until your hands are **completely dry** — at least 5 minutes — before handling any receipts.

## Part 5: The Full Protection Toolkit

### The Dry Hands Rule
Never handle receipts when your hands are wet from sanitizer or lotion. Plain soap and water is far safer — it cleans without compromising your skin's protective barrier.

### Backside-Only Handling
The bisphenol coating is on the shiny printed side of the receipt. Grab receipts from the edges or the unprinted back side, and fold them with the printed side facing inward. This one habit alone significantly reduces your daily exposure.

### Nitrile Gloves
If you want a complete barrier, use [nitrile gloves](/resources) — the blue or black ones. Clear vinyl or latex gloves are not effective against bisphenols; the chemicals can penetrate them. Look for thin, high-dexterity nitrile gloves designed for food service — they won't slow you down at the register.

## Part 6: How to Talk to Your Manager

In 2026, [Washington State](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=70A.222) enacted a full ban on bisphenols in thermal paper — the most comprehensive receipt chemical legislation in the US to date. California's AB 1604 is pending, which would extend similar protections statewide.

These laws exist because the science is clear: bisphenol exposure from receipts is a real, measurable risk that falls disproportionately on retail workers.

You can ask your manager to make three changes:

**Switch to phenol-free paper.** This is the only truly safe option. It costs roughly the same as conventional thermal paper.

**Look for Blue4est paper.** It has a distinctive blue-grey tint and uses zero bisphenols — a simple way to verify your store has made the switch.

**Encourage digital receipts.** The safest receipt is no receipt. Email and text receipts eliminate the exposure entirely and cost the store nothing extra.
